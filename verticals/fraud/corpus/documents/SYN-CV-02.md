# CV-02 — Guidance: Reimbursement and Redress for Victims of Authorised Payment Fraud

**Issued by the Authority · Customer Vulnerability series · Reference CV-02**

## 1. Status and application

1.1 This guidance describes the Authority's expectations of relevant
firms when assessing reimbursement and redress for customers who have
authorised payments as a result of the schemes described in TY-02. It
applies the vulnerability principles of CV-01 to the specific context
of redress. It does not address unauthorised transactions, which are
governed by the Framework's payment provisions directly.

## 2. The starting point

2.1 The starting point under the Framework is reimbursement of a
customer defrauded into authorising a payment, subject to the limited
grounds in §3. Firms should build their assessment processes from
that starting point, not toward it: a process designed to find
reasons to decline will find them, and the Authority reads decline
rates and reasons across firms.

2.2 The sending and receiving firms share responsibility for the
loss in the proportions the Framework provides. A receiving firm's
share reflects the account-opening and network-detection expectations
of SG-01 and TY-01; receiving firms should not treat reimbursement as
a sending-side matter.

## 3. The exception and its limits

3.1 A firm may decline or reduce reimbursement only where the
customer acted with gross negligence in the authorisation, assessed
against what that customer, in their circumstances, could reasonably
have been expected to recognise. Gross negligence is a high bar: it
is not met by the customer having missed warnings, ignored friction,
or believed a competent deception.

3.2 The assessment is customer-specific. Vulnerability at the time of
payment, on the indicators described in CV-01 §2 and §3, is weighed
whether or not the customer raised it: a firm that held the
indicators in its own data cannot decline on a standard the customer
could not meet. The cultivation and coercion patterns of TY-02 §3.3
and §4 will rarely if ever sit alongside a sustainable gross
negligence finding.

3.3 The quality of the firm's own intervention bears on the
assessment. Where the firm's contact merely re-confirmed
authorisation (the practice criticised in TR-01 §4.3), the firm
should not rely on the customer having "proceeded after warning".
Where a properly conducted intervention of the kind described in
CV-01 §4 was recorded and the customer proceeded, that record is
weighed — in the manner DP-01 §2.1 describes for records generally.

## 4. Process expectations

4.1 Assessments should be completed within the Framework's stated
period, with the customer informed of the outcome and the reasons in
terms they can understand and challenge. A decline letter that
recites the exception without engaging with the customer's
circumstances does not meet this expectation.

4.2 The customer should not carry the burden of investigation. The
firm holds the transaction data, the intervention records and the
network picture (TY-01 §5.2); the customer holds their account of
events. Processes should be designed accordingly, and repeated
re-narration avoided for the reasons in CV-01 §5.1.

4.3 A reimbursement decision does not affect the firm's reporting
obligations, which will already have arisen or not on the SG-02 §3
test. Firms should not delay reporting pending the reimbursement
outcome, and should not treat reimbursement as closing a matter that
remains reportable (DL-01 §3.4).

## 5. Learning from redress outcomes

5.1 Redress outcomes are calibration data. Confirmed-fraud
populations assembled for reimbursement purposes should feed the
monitoring calibration cycle described in TR-01 §5.1 and the cohort
analysis of SG-01 §5; a firm that reimburses losses without feeding
the learning loop pays twice.

5.2 Boards should see redress volumes, decline rates, decline
grounds and overturn rates on appeal within the annual analysis
described in SG-02 §6.1, with vulnerable-customer outcomes
distinguished per CV-01 §6.1.

**End of CV-02.**
