# TY-04 — Typology Report: Money Mule Networks — Reporting Obligations and Account Exit

**Issued by the Authority · Typology series · Reference TY-04**

## 1. Purpose

1.1 TY-01 describes how money mule networks are structured and how
their accounts behave. This report addresses what follows from
detection: the legal position of a firm that finds itself operating
accounts inside a network, the disclosure obligations that arise,
and the practices around inhibiting and exiting mule accounts
observed in matters reported to the Authority. The two reports are
companions; nothing here restates the structural material in TY-01.

1.2 The report is directed at the decisions a firm must make in the
window between suspicion forming and the account being closed. That
window is where reported matters most often go wrong: the detection
was sound, and the response squandered it.

## 2. The legal position of the operating firm

2.1 Funds moving through a mule account are, on the facts this
typology describes, criminal property. A firm that continues to
execute transfers from an account it suspects to be a mule account
is at risk of facilitating the concealment, disguise, conversion or
transfer of criminal property within the meaning of section 327 of
the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 (the statutory extract at OGL-0003).
The protection available to the firm lies in the disclosure and
consent machinery of that Act, which is why the disclosure decision
and the inhibition decision cannot be separated in practice.

2.2 For staff in the regulated sector the position is sharpened by
section 330 of the same Act (the extract at OGL-0004): failure to
disclose knowledge or suspicion of money laundering, where the
information came to them in the course of regulated business, is
itself an offence. A firm's escalation arrangements exist so that
suspicion formed anywhere in the firm reaches its nominated officer;
arrangements that let suspicion sit unescalated in a queue expose
the individuals in that chain, not only the firm.

2.3 Two consequences follow for control design. First, the
threshold question for escalation is suspicion, not proof: staff
who wait for certainty before escalating have waited too long, on
the statutory language itself. Second, the firm's records must be
able to show when suspicion crystallised and what was done next,
because both the section 327 risk and the section 330 risk turn on
what was known when.

## 3. Inhibition

3.1 On detection of a suspected mule account the operating firm
faces a three-way tension: continuing to operate the account risks
further movement of criminal property; inhibiting it risks tipping
off the network; and acting against a genuine customer on a false
positive causes real harm, particularly where the account holder is
a coerced or deceived recruit of the kind described at TY-01 §3.

3.2 Observed good practice resolves the tension in favour of
prompt inhibition of outbound movement once suspicion is
documented, with the account held pending the disclosure process
rather than closed outright. Inhibition of outbound payments
preserves funds for recovery and stops the account doing further
work for the network; it is reversible if the suspicion resolves
benignly, in a way that closure and denunciation are not.

3.3 The Authority's expectations for the speed of inhibition
decisions, and its findings on firms' actual performance against
those expectations, are examined in the money mule thematic review
in the TR series. This report deliberately confines itself to the
typology and the decision structure; the review carries the
supervisory assessment.

## 4. The exit decision

4.1 Exit — termination of the relationship — is a distinct decision
from inhibition, and reported matters show it done both too slowly
and too crudely. Done too slowly, second-generation accounts of the
kind described at TY-01 §2.3 survive first detection and continue
aggregating. Done too crudely, coerced first-generation holders are
exited with a fraud marker whose consequences follow them for
years, in circumstances where the firm's own file showed the
indicators of coercion that CV-01 asks firms to act on.

4.2 The Authority expects exit decisions to distinguish the
network's controllers and knowing participants from its recruited
and deceived instruments. The distinction is drawn from the file
the firm already holds: recruitment indicators at onboarding,
coaching indicators in interactions, and the account's position in
the flow. A first-generation account opened with the holder's own
long-established identity, showing coached answers and no share of
the extracted value, presents differently from an aggregation
account operated under a synthetic identity, and the exit
treatment should differ accordingly.

## 5. What detection is for

5.1 The network signals available to an operating firm — the
convergence patterns at TY-01 §5.2 above all — are most valuable
when they travel. A single firm's exit of a single account imposes
little cost on a network that treats first-generation accounts as
disposable; disclosure that lets the pattern be assembled across
firms is what imposes real cost. The transaction monitoring
thematic review at TR-01 found, at §3.4, that the detection
arrangements feeding this process fail most often silently, at the
data layer; a firm confident in its mule detection should first be
confident its detection is seeing the account population at all.

5.2 Firms should accordingly treat their mule caseload as a
calibration input of the kind TR-01 §5.1 describes: each confirmed
network account is evidence about which of the indicators at TY-01
§4 and §5 their own arrangements actually caught, which they
missed, and where in the lifecycle detection landed. A firm whose
confirmed cases are all detected at the second generation has
learned that its first-generation controls are decorative.

## 6. Use of this report

6.1 This report should be read with TY-01, with the account opening
and monitoring expectations at SG-01, and with the reporting
practice material at SG-02. Its statutory references are to the
extracts at OGL-0003 and OGL-0004; firms should take their own
advice on the application of those provisions to specific facts.
The supervisory findings on inhibition and exit performance are in
the TR series review of mule detection and account exit.
