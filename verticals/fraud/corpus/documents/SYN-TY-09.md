# TY-09 — Typology Report: Insider-Enabled Fraud

**Issued by the Authority · Typology series · Reference TY-09**

## 1. Purpose

1.1 This report describes fraud enabled from inside the firm: by
employees, contractors and outsourced staff who use their access,
their knowledge of controls, or their position in a process to
commit fraud or to serve those who do. Every typology in this
series has an insider-enabled variant, and the enabling is the
common structure this report addresses; the external mechanics are
covered where each typology is described.

1.2 The Authority's experience is that firms model insider risk as
a personnel integrity problem and control it at recruitment. The
reported matters underlying this report say otherwise: the typical
insider was honestly recruited, and either developed motive in
post — debt, grievance, coercion — or was cultivated by an external
network precisely because their access had become valuable. The
risk lives in the access, and moves with it.

## 2. The statutory frame

2.1 The insider's position is squarely within fraud by abuse of
position under section 4 of the Fraud Act 2006 (the statutory
extract at OGL-0002): an employee occupies a position in which they
are expected to safeguard, or not to act against, the financial
interests of the firm and its customers, and the dishonest abuse of
that position with intent to gain or cause loss completes the
offence. Section 4(2) of the extract deserves particular attention
in control design: the abuse may consist of an omission rather than
an act. The analyst who recognises a mule network and chooses not
to escalate it, for payment or under coercion, is within the
section's contemplation without having pressed a key.

## 3. What insiders sell

3.1 Reported matters show three commodities. The first is data:
customer identities, balances, and above all the contact and
security details that make the impersonation typologies
convincing — a fraudster who knows the customer's recent
transactions sounds like the bank. The second is action: payments
released, limits raised, markers removed, addresses changed,
accounts opened against policy. The third, least visible and most
damaging, is control intelligence: which patterns the monitoring
catches, which thresholds trigger review, which checks are
performed on which queues, and when. A network holding current
control intelligence designs its activity to pass, and the firm's
detection statistics stay green while its losses move.

3.2 The mule networks described in TY-01 and TY-04 are systematic
consumers of all three. Recruited insiders open accounts that pass
onboarding because the insider knows what onboarding checks;
inhibition decisions leak before they execute; and exit
investigations of the kind TY-04 §4 describes are quietly starved
of the file evidence they turn on. A firm working a significant
mule caseload should treat unexplained investigative failure —
evidence missing, subjects forewarned, patterns going quiet on
review dates — as an insider indicator in itself.

## 4. Indicators

4.1 Access anomalies lead: lookups of accounts with no case or
service justification, especially clustered around subsequently
defrauded customers; access at abnormal hours or from abnormal
locations; privilege use far from the employee's queue; and
searches structured like reconnaissance — sequences of high-value
customers, or of accounts later appearing in one network.

4.2 Process anomalies follow: overrides and exceptions
concentrated in one operator; quality-check pass rates implausibly
clean; decisions consistently favourable to accounts later
confirmed as network accounts; and work orchestrated so that
particular items route to particular staff.

4.3 The joining discipline of this series applies here with a
complication: the data that would reveal the pattern — access
logs, case outcomes, override records — is typically distributed
across systems owned by the same functions the insider may sit in.
Effective insider analytics therefore require an access-controlled
joining capability deliberately separated from operational teams,
and reported matters include cases where the monitoring existed
and the insider was among its administrators.

## 5. Consequence management

5.1 Confirmed insider fraud engages the firm's disclosure
obligations as any fraud does, and additionally engages the
Authority's interest in the firm's systems and controls: an
insider who operated for years is evidence about the controls,
not only about the individual. The Authority's approach to
enforcement where systems and controls failings are found,
including those surfaced by insider matters, is set out in the
decision procedure on enforcement for systems and controls
failings at DP-03. Firms should expect an insider matter to be
examined in both dimensions, and should conduct their internal
investigation to a standard that serves both.

## 6. Use of this report

6.1 Firms should map the three commodities at §3.1 against their
own access model, test whether the indicators at §4 are visible in
their data at all, and examine the independence of whatever
insider analytics they operate, per §4.3. This report is
descriptive; the constant across reported matters is that the
access was the asset, and controls that follow the access —
joiners, movers, leavers, and above all accumulators — age better
than controls that follow the org chart.
