# SG-09 — Sector Guidance: Account Restriction and Exit Where Criminal Property Is Suspected

**Issued by the Authority · Sector Guidance series · Reference SG-09**

## 1. Status and application

1.1 This guidance applies to relevant firms deciding what to do with
an account and the funds in it once the firm suspects that some or
all of the balance is criminal property: whether to restrict the
account, whether to continue operating it, and whether and how to
terminate the relationship and release the funds. It describes how
the Authority interprets the Framework's obligations as they bear on
those decisions, and what it will look for in supervisory work. It
is guidance, not rules: a firm may meet the underlying obligations
by other means, but should expect to explain how.

1.2 The statutory extract at OGL-0003 is section 327 of the Proceeds
of Crime Act 2002: the offence of concealing, disguising,
converting, transferring or removing criminal property, and the
circumstances in which a person does not commit it. Section 327
refers outward at four points to material that is not among the
Framework's extracts — the authorised disclosure and appropriate
consent machinery, at section 327(2)(a); the threshold amount, at
section 327(2C)(b) and section 327(2D)(c); the customer due
diligence duties, at section 327(2E)(c); and the provisions on
tipping off. This guidance stops at each of those edges. It states
no rule about the consent process, about how a threshold amount is
determined, or about what a firm may say to a customer. It is not a
statement of a firm's obligations under that Act, and firms should
take their own advice on them.

1.3 This guidance is concerned with the account and the funds. The
customer-facing response to suspected fraud runs concurrently with
the decisions described here and is addressed at SG-05; the
obligations that guidance describes are not suspended while these
decisions are taken, and this guidance adds no treatment step of its
own. Where a decision under this guidance generates a reporting
consequence, this guidance states the trigger and SG-06 governs what
the report contains. The internal escalation of suspicion is
addressed at SG-02.

## 2. The risk this guidance addresses

2.1 The decision this guidance addresses has a cost in both
directions, and neither direction is the safe default. A balance
restricted is a customer denied their money — a customer who may be
neither a wrongdoer nor connected to one, and who bears the
consequence immediately while the firm resolves its position at its
own pace. A balance released or handed back may be an act the firm
was not entitled to do. Firms that treat one of those costs as the
only real one make the same mistake in opposite directions.

2.2 The statute does not assume that an account is wholly clean or
wholly tainted. Section 327(2F)(c) is drafted for the firm that
knows or suspects that part but not all of the funds is criminal
property, and section 327(2F)(d) for the case where the part cannot
be identified at the time. That is the ordinary condition of a
suspected account, not an exotic one. A firm whose procedures offer
only the two absolute answers is answering a question the statute
does not pose, and will reach for closure and exit in cases that did
not require them.

2.3 The typology work records the same tension from the other side.
TY-04 §3.1 describes the three-way bind facing a firm that detects a
suspected mule account: continuing to operate it risks further
movement of criminal property, inhibiting it risks tipping off the
network, and acting on a false positive causes real harm to a
genuine customer. That bind is not particular to mule accounts. It
is the general shape of the decision this guidance addresses, and it
is why the guidance is concerned with how the decision is made,
scoped and recorded rather than with prescribing its outcome.

## 3. The decision and its exposure

3.1 Section 327(1) makes it an offence for a person to conceal,
disguise, convert or transfer criminal property, or to remove it
from the jurisdiction. Two of those acts — converting and
transferring — are performed by a firm in the ordinary operation of
a payment account; a third, removing, is engaged where funds leave
the jurisdiction. This is what makes the handling of a suspected
balance a decision with legal consequence rather than an
administrative step, and why the Authority expects it to be taken
deliberately, by someone identified, on a basis the firm can state.

3.2 Section 327(2C) addresses the firm that continues to operate the
account. A deposit-taking body, electronic money institution or
payment institution that converts or transfers criminal property
does not commit the offence if it does the act in operating an
account maintained with it and the value of the criminal property
concerned is less than the threshold amount. The provision reaches
converting and transferring only; it does not reach removal from the
jurisdiction. How a threshold amount is determined is outside this
guidance, for the reason at §1.2.

3.3 Section 327(2F) addresses the mixed balance, and reaches
converting, transferring and removing. It applies where the firm is
carrying on business in the regulated sector; the act is done in the
course of that business, on behalf of the customer, in operating an
account maintained with the firm or in connection with holding
property for the customer; the firm knows or suspects that part but
not all of the funds is criminal property; it is not possible at the
time to identify which part; and — the condition firms most often
overlook — the value of the funds is not, as a direct or indirect
result of the act, less than the value of the suspected criminal
property at the time of the act. Where the provision applies,
section 327(2G) assumes the funds remaining immediately after the
act to include that property. The assumption is not a permission: it
describes the position the firm is left in, and a firm that has
relied on the provision still holds a balance treated as containing
the suspected property.

3.4 Where the firm's route is disclosure, section 327(2)(a) provides
that a person does not commit the offence if he makes an authorised
disclosure and, where the disclosure precedes the act, has the
appropriate consent. This guidance goes no further into that
machinery, for the reason at §1.2. What follows for the firm's
reporting is at §5.3.

## 4. Restriction while the position is resolved

4.1 A restriction is a decision and should be recorded as one: what
is restricted, on what suspicion, by whom, and when it will be
considered again. The Authority expects every restriction to carry a
stated review interval. A restriction with no scheduled review is
not a decision but a state, and a firm that cannot say when it will
next consider the matter has in substance decided to leave the
customer without their money until something else happens.

4.2 Restriction should reach as far as the suspicion reaches and no
further. A suspicion attaching to one payment stream, one
counterparty or one channel does not of itself justify restricting a
whole relationship. Where a firm restricts more widely than the
suspicion, the reason belongs on the record; the Authority's
question will be not only what was restricted but why that scope.

4.3 Restriction and termination are different acts and should not be
collapsed into one. TY-04 §3.2 records the practice observed to work
in mule matters: inhibition of outbound movement once suspicion is
documented, with the account held pending the disclosure process
rather than closed outright — a step that preserves funds for
recovery and is reversible if the suspicion resolves benignly, in a
way that closure is not. The Authority regards that reversibility as
one of restriction's principal merits. A firm that moves directly to
exit forgoes it, and should be able to say why.

4.4 The obligations at SG-05 run throughout this period. What the
firm owes the customer while its position is resolved is that
guidance's subject, and nothing here adds to it or displaces it.
What may be said to the customer about a restriction is addressed at
SG-02 and, in part, by provisions outside the Framework's extracts;
this guidance states no rule on it.

## 5. Exit and the return of funds

5.1 Termination is a distinct decision carrying its own exposure.
Returning a customer's money is a transfer within the meaning of
section 327(1), and a firm that exits a relationship by handing back
a balance it suspects has done an act the section describes.

5.2 Section 327(2D) addresses that act. A person carrying on
business in the regulated sector that is not excluded business does
not commit the offence by transferring or handing over to a customer
money or other property of, or owing to, the customer, where the act
is done for the purposes of the termination of the business
relationship, the total value so transferred is less than the
threshold amount, and the person has complied with the customer due
diligence duties before the act is done. Two features deserve
attention. The provision is tied to the purpose of termination: it
is an exit provision, not a general permission to return suspected
funds. And section 327(2E)(b) provides that transferring or handing
over to the customer includes transferring at the customer's
direction to another person — so a customer's instruction to send
the balance to an account held elsewhere falls inside the provision
rather than around it. Firms should not treat the destination of the
funds as the thing that makes the act safe.

5.3 An exit does not resolve the firm's reporting position. Where
the matter generates a reporting consequence, that consequence is
triggered by the suspicion and is not discharged by the closure.
SG-06 §2.2 records the failure this guidance is concerned to
prevent: the loss absorbed as a dispute cost, the customer exited
quietly, the conduct left invisible to the next firm the actor
approaches. What a report must contain, and when it must be made,
are matters for SG-06 and are not restated here.

5.4 Exit should distinguish between those against whom the suspicion
is properly directed and those caught up in the matter. TY-04 §4.2
draws that distinction for mule accounts — the network's controllers
and knowing participants on one side, its recruited and deceived
instruments on the other — from the file the firm already holds. The
general point holds beyond that typology. An exit executed
identically in every case is not a decision about this customer, and
the consequences of a crude exit follow the customer for years after
the firm's file is closed.

## 6. Supervisory consequence and records

6.1 Where supervisory work identifies a failure in the handling of a
suspected balance, what the Authority weighs in deciding its
response is set out at DP-01 §2, and the pathway from finding to
formal action at DP-01 §3. The assessment is made on the records the
firm kept: DP-01 §2.1 records that a firm whose records show the
indicators present, the action taken and the rationale will be
assessed on those records, and a firm without such records on the
absence.

6.2 The distinction at DP-01 §2.2 between control failures and
outcome events applies with particular force here. A balance
released and later shown to have been criminal property does not of
itself establish a failure; the question is whether the firm's
arrangements were adequate and operated as designed. The converse
holds equally: a restriction that happened to be correct, imposed
without a basis the firm can now show, is not evidence of a
functioning arrangement. Where the failing identified is one of
capability rather than candour, the remediation route at DP-01 §4 is
available.

6.3 Firms should retain, for each account restricted or exited under
this guidance, records supporting later reconstruction: the
suspicion and its basis, and when it formed; the balance held and
what part of it the firm suspected; what was restricted, on what
scope, and the reviews conducted; whether the firm understood itself
to be relying on a provision of section 327 and, if so, which
conditions it took to be satisfied and on what information; the
termination decision and the destination of the funds; and the
timing of each step. The last is not a formality. Every provision
described at §3 turns on what the firm knew, and on the value of the
property, at the time of the act — and a record assembled afterwards
that fixes neither to a time cannot support the position the firm
took.
