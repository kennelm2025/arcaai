# TY-02 — Typology Report: Authorised Push Payment Fraud and Coerced Authorisation

**Issued by the Authority · Typology series · Reference TY-02**

## 1. Purpose

1.1 This report describes the principal scenarios in which customers
of relevant firms are induced to authorise payments to fraudsters. It
complements TY-01, which addresses the account networks that receive
such payments; this report addresses the sending side — how
authorisation is obtained. The Authority's expectations of firms
facing these scenarios are set out in DL-01 and, for intervention
practice, TR-01 §4.

## 2. Why authorised fraud defeats naive controls

2.1 In every scenario in this report, the payment is genuinely
authorised by the account holder. Controls built on the question "did
the customer authorise this?" therefore pass it. The operative
questions are different: does the payment fit the customer; does the
payee make sense; and was the customer, at the moment of
authorisation, acting on a false picture of reality constructed by
another person.

2.2 Fraudsters engineer urgency precisely because reflection is
fatal to the scheme. Effective controls insert reflection at the
moment it is least wanted — which is why the Authority regards
pre-release intervention (TR-01 §4.2) as materially more effective
than after-the-event recovery, and why intervention scripts that
merely re-confirm authorisation achieve nothing (TR-01 §4.3).

## 3. Principal scenarios

3.1 **Impersonation of the firm or of authority.** The customer is
contacted by a person claiming to be their firm's fraud team, or an
official body, and told their funds are at risk and must be moved to
a "safe account". The scheme inverts the firm's own protective
language and frequently follows a small genuine-looking trigger
engineered by the fraudster. Distinguishing marks: inbound contact
the firm did not make; instructions to ignore or mislead the firm's
own warnings; a destination account unknown to the customer minutes
before.

3.2 **Purchase and advance-fee schemes.** The customer pays for
goods, services or opportunities that do not exist. Value is
typically lower than in impersonation cases but volume is higher.
Distinguishing marks: first payment to a newly added payee (the
combination examined at TR-01 §3.3); price or return materially
better than market; pressure to move off-platform and pay by direct
transfer.

3.3 **Romance and long-cultivation schemes.** Authorisation follows
weeks or months of relationship-building. Payments are frequently
repeated, escalating, and rationalised by the customer, who may
actively resist intervention. These cases present the strongest
overlap with the vulnerability indicators at CV-01 §2 and the
coercion indicators at CV-01 §3; staff conducting intervention calls
should expect the customer to advocate for the fraudster.

3.4 **Invoice and payee-manipulation schemes.** A legitimate payment
obligation is redirected by altering payee details, typically through
compromised correspondence. The customer intends to pay a real
creditor; only the destination is false. Distinguishing marks: a
changed account for an established payee; payment instructions
arriving by a channel or at a cadence that departs from the
relationship's history.

## 4. Coercion as a distinct pattern

4.1 In a minority of reported cases the customer is not deceived but
coerced: authorisation is obtained through threat or control by a
person known to the customer. These cases rarely present the urgency
signature of 3.1 and frequently present none of the payee-novelty
signals; what they present is a payment pattern inconsistent with the
customer's interests alongside the relationship indicators described
at CV-01 §3. Firms should not assume that the absence of scam
markers means the absence of harm.

4.2 Coerced first-generation account access — the overlap between
this report and TY-01 §3 — means a single customer may
simultaneously be a victim on the sending side and a recruited mule
on the receiving side. Firms should resist classifying such
customers by whichever role was detected first.

## 5. Use of this report

5.1 Firms should map their detection and intervention arrangements
against the scenarios in this report, scenario by scenario, and
should expect the Authority's supervisory work following DL-01 to
probe that mapping. Reporting obligations under SG-02 arise on the
usual test in every scenario above; the customer's authorisation of
the payment is irrelevant to whether the firm's reporting threshold
is met.

**End of TY-02.**
