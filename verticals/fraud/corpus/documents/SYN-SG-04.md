# SG-04 — Sector Guidance: Payment Journey Interventions for Authorised Push Payment Fraud

**Issued by the Authority · Sector Guidance series · Reference SG-04**

## 1. Status and application

1.1 This guidance applies to relevant firms executing
customer-instructed payments from retail payment accounts. It
describes how the Authority interprets the Framework's obligations
as they bear on the payment journey — the sequence from payee
creation to the release of funds — where the customer authorising
the payment may be doing so under deception. It is guidance, not
rules: a firm may meet the underlying obligations by other means,
but should expect to explain how.

1.2 This guidance is concerned with the sending side of authorised
push payment fraud: the position of the firm whose customer is
deceived into instructing a payment. The receiving side, and the
monitoring of accounts in operation generally, are addressed
elsewhere in the Framework. This guidance is likewise not a
statement of any firm's obligations in respect of reimbursement,
which are addressed in the applicable scheme rules.

1.3 The distinguishing difficulty of this fraud class is that every
formal control can pass. The instruction is genuine, the
authentication succeeds, and the customer means to make the payment.
The controls this guidance describes are accordingly directed not at
the authenticity of the instruction but at the circumstances in
which it was formed.

## 2. The risk this guidance addresses

2.1 The typology work at TY-03 describes how authorised push payment
fraud operates: the deceptions employed, the pretexts under which
urgency is manufactured, and the routes by which victims are brought
to the point of payment (TY-03 §2). The Authority's thematic review
of payment journey controls at TR-03 examines firms' practice in
this area; this guidance sets out the expectations against which
that practice will be assessed.

2.2 The deception at the centre of this fraud class is, in the
ordinary case, an offence under section 2 of the Fraud Act 2006 (the
extract at OGL-0001): a dishonest false representation made with
intent to cause gain or loss. Section 2(5) of that Act makes clear
that a representation may be made by submission to a system or
device — the fraudster's misrepresentations reach the customer, and
sometimes the firm's own channels, without any human intermediary.
Firms should not treat the digital character of the deception as
diminishing its significance; the statute does not.

2.3 The customer is the target of the deception, but the firm is not
a bystander to it. The firm operates the journey along which the
deception must travel to succeed, and it holds signals about the
payment, the payee and the customer's behaviour that the customer,
acting under the fraudster's direction, may be unable to weigh. The
Authority's expectation is that firms design the payment journey so
that those signals are brought to bear before funds are released.

## 3. The payment journey as a control surface

3.1 The creation of a new payee is the single most informative event
in the journey and should be treated as such. The sending-side
indicators described at TY-03 §4 cluster around it: a first payment
to a newly created payee, at a value inconsistent with the account's
history, often following contact the firm did not originate. The
first payment to a newly created payee is where these indicators
concentrate, and firms should treat it as the point of greatest
scrutiny, friction and communication in the journey.

3.2 Friction should be proportionate to indication, not uniform.
Journey-wide friction applied indiscriminately trains customers to
click through it, and a warning the customer has seen on every
payment protects no one on the payment that matters. Firms should
reserve their strongest interventions for payments carrying the
indicator patterns at TY-03 §4, and should be able to evidence how
the strength of intervention varies with the strength of indication.

3.3 Warnings should be specific to the pretext, not generic to
fraud. The typology work records the small number of pretexts under
which most losses occur (TY-03 §2); a warning that names the pretext
the payment resembles — and states plainly what the firm will never
ask a customer to do — gives the customer information capable of
breaking the deception. A generic caution does not. Firms should
maintain their warning content against the current typology and
should be able to show when it was last revised and why.

3.4 The journey should be designed on the expectation that the
fraudster is present in it. In a material fraction of cases
described at TY-03 §2, the customer is on a call, in a message
thread, or following written instructions from the fraudster while
transacting, and will be coached through the firm's questions and
warnings. Interventions that ask the customer to confirm their own
uncoerced intent are systematically defeated by live coaching and
should not be relied upon as the primary control where indication is
high; interventions that interrupt the coaching — a delay the
fraudster cannot sustain, an outbound contact on a channel the
fraudster does not control — are stronger, and firms should prefer
them where indication warrants.

## 4. Interruption, delay and release

4.1 Where the indicators present at the point of instruction warrant
it, the Authority expects firms to be willing to delay or decline
release of a payment the customer has authorised. A firm that treats
a formally valid instruction as extinguishing its ability to
intervene has designed its journey around the fraud's central
mechanism. The firm's judgement in these cases should be exercised,
recorded at the time, and capable of later reconstruction, in the
same discipline the firm applies to any other risk decision.

4.2 Delay is an intervention with a purpose, not a pause. Its
purpose is to separate the customer from the live deception and to
bring an independent signal — human or otherwise — to bear that the
journey itself could not. A delay that expires into automatic
release, without contact or reassessment, has added inconvenience
without protection.

4.3 The typology work at TY-03 §5 records that this fraud class
interacts with customer vulnerability: the pretexts are chosen for,
and pressed hardest against, customers least placed to resist them.
The treatment of vulnerability in firms' fraud controls is addressed
elsewhere in the Framework; for the purposes of this guidance, firms
should recognise that the indicator patterns at TY-03 §4 may present
differently across their customer base, and that calibration against
a single behavioural norm will underweight exactly the customers the
typology identifies.

## 5. Learning from outcomes

5.1 Firms should analyse confirmed authorised push payment fraud by
journey cohort: the payee-creation route, the channel of
instruction, the warnings shown, the interventions applied and the
point at which each control passed. The principle is that applied to
onboarding cohorts in SG-01 §5, directed at the journey: where a
cohort of journeys generates disproportionate losses, the firm
should adjust the controls on that cohort and be able to evidence
the adjustment.

5.2 A firm unable to say which of its journey configurations its
losses travel through, or whether its warnings are read, has not
completed the feedback loop this guidance expects. The Authority
will ask for this analysis in supervisory work.

## 6. Records

6.1 Firms should retain, for each material payment decision in scope
of this guidance, the indicators present, the interventions applied,
the customer's responses and the basis of release or refusal, in a
form that supports later reconstruction, on the principle applied in
SG-01 §6. Where a confirmed fraud follows a completed journey, the
Authority's first questions will concern what the journey showed the
customer, what the firm's indicators registered, and why the payment
was released.
