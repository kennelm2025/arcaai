# TY-01 — Typology Report: Money Mule Networks in Retail Payments

**Issued by the Authority · Typology series · Reference TY-01**

## 1. Purpose

1.1 This report describes the structure and operation of money mule
networks as observed in matters reported to the Authority, so that
relevant firms can recognise the patterns in their own data. It is
descriptive, not exhaustive: networks adapt, and a firm that controls
only for the patterns in this report will be controlling for last
year's networks.

1.2 Control expectations arising from these typologies are set out in
the account opening guidance (SG-01) and were tested in the
transaction monitoring thematic review (TR-01). Reporting obligations
where these patterns are observed are covered by SG-02 §3 and §4.3.

## 2. Network structure

2.1 Observed networks are layered. First-generation accounts receive
funds directly from victims; second-generation accounts aggregate
from multiple first-generation accounts; extraction occurs from the
second layer onward through cash withdrawal, cryptoasset purchase, or
cross-border transfer. The layering exists to defeat recovery: by the
time a victim's firm traces the first hop, value has usually left the
second.

2.2 First-generation accounts are treated by the network as
disposable. They are used for days, sometimes hours. The economics of
the network assume their loss; controls that merely close
first-generation accounts after use impose little cost on the
network.

2.3 Second-generation and later accounts are the network's working
capital and are operated with more care: longer aging, plausible
activity patterns between fraud flows, balances kept below attention
thresholds. Detection effort directed at this layer imposes real
cost.

## 3. Recruitment of account holders

3.1 Recruitment routes observed in reported matters include
employment offers requiring use of the recruit's own account to
"process payments"; social media solicitation offering payment for
account access; and coercion of individuals known to the recruiter.
A material share of first-generation account holders in reported
matters show indicators of vulnerability, coercion or deception about
the nature of the arrangement; CV-01 §3 addresses how firms should
respond when these indicators surface.

3.2 Recruited holders are frequently instructed in how to answer
firm questions — at onboarding, during intervention calls, and after
funds arrive. Scripted or coached answers are themselves an
indicator, particularly where the customer's account activity is
inconsistent with the explanation given (SG-01 §3.4).

## 4. Onboarding-stage indicators

4.1 Networks require volumes of accounts, and acquisition at volume
leaves traces: clusters of applications sharing devices, network
addresses, contact-detail patterns or document characteristics;
applications whose digital footprint is thin relative to the claimed
identity; applicants who struggle with information a genuine holder
of the claimed identity would know.

4.2 No single indicator is conclusive, and the Authority does not
expect firms to decline applications on isolated signals. It expects
clustering to be visible: a firm should be able to detect that it has
received forty applications sharing a device fingerprint, and the
review at TR-01 §3.4 illustrates what happens when the relevant data
feeds silently fail.

## 5. Post-opening behaviour

5.1 The characteristic first-generation lifecycle is: a quiet period
after opening; a first material inbound credit inconsistent with
prior activity; rapid onward movement, frequently within hours, to
one or a small number of destinations; repetition until the account
is closed or abandoned. SG-01 §4.2 sets the Authority's expectation
that this pattern prompts intervention rather than observation, and
SG-01 §4.3 explains why quiet aging defeats time-based graduation.

5.2 Second-generation accounts show many-to-one inbound convergence:
credits from multiple unrelated payers with no plausible common
cause. Convergence is among the strongest network signals available
to a receiving firm and is invisible to the sending firm — which is
why fragmentary reporting of visible networks (SG-02 §4.3) wastes
the one vantage point that can see it.

## 6. Use of this report

6.1 Firms should test their detection arrangements against the
patterns described here and treat the exercise as calibration input
of the kind described in TR-01 §5.1. The Authority will update this
typology series as observed behaviour changes; TY-02 addresses the
distinct patterns around coerced and deceived payment authorisation.

**End of TY-01.**
