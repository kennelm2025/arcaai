# TY-08 — Typology Report: First-Party and Friendly Fraud

**Issued by the Authority · Typology series · Reference TY-08**

## 1. Purpose

1.1 This report describes fraud committed by the account holder
themselves: false claims of unauthorised activity, false disputes
of genuine purchases, abuse of goodwill and reimbursement
processes, and first-party participation in schemes the rest of
this series treats as third-party. It completes the series'
coverage of the question every disputed transaction poses — who is
lying — by addressing the cases where the answer is the customer.

1.2 The report's operational significance is double-edged, and the
Authority states both edges plainly. Firms under-detect this
typology, because reimbursement and dispute processes are built on
a presumption of customer honesty that first-party actors exploit.
And firms over-suspect it, because first-party fraud is the
convenient explanation for any claim a firm would prefer not to
pay, and a genuine victim treated as a fraudster is among the worst
outcomes a firm's fraud operation can produce.

## 2. The shape of the fraud

2.1 The false unauthorised claim is the core variant: the customer
makes or knowingly permits a payment, then reports it as
unauthorised to obtain a refund. The claim that a transaction the
customer performed was not performed by them is a false
representation to the firm under section 2 of the Fraud Act 2006
(the extract at OGL-0001), made to make a gain — the refund — or to
cause the firm loss; the statutory analysis is no different because
the claimant is a customer.

2.2 Adjacent variants observed in reported matters include false
non-receipt and quality disputes against genuine merchant
transactions; staged or exaggerated fraud claims timed to financial
distress; goodwill farming, in which small false claims are spread
across products and firms below investigation thresholds; and the
deliberate briefing of a genuine payment as an APP scam claim, in
the expectation of reimbursement treatment.

2.3 First-party conduct also appears inside the third-party
typologies. The first-generation mule who claims their account was
taken over is running a first-party defence of third-party conduct;
TY-01 §3 describes the genuinely coerced version of the same
account holder, and distinguishing the two is the same file-based
judgement the exit material in this series describes. Complicit
"victims" also appear in staged APP frauds, splitting the refund
with the supposed fraudster.

## 3. Indicators

3.1 The indicator families are historical and behavioural rather
than transactional, because the transaction itself is genuine.
Claim history is first among them: repeat claims across products,
claims at multiple firms visible through industry data sharing,
and claim timing correlated with balance stress. Device and
session evidence is second: an "unauthorised" transaction from the
customer's enrolled device, at their usual location, inside a
session showing their established behavioural patterns, is a claim
contradicted by the firm's own records. Conduct around the claim
is third: refusal to support a police report, rehearsed narrative
detail, and settlement-seeking behaviour when investigation begins.

3.2 No single indicator convicts, and §1.2's second edge governs:
device evidence in particular is exactly what a remote-access
takeover of the kind TY-07 describes also produces, and a SIM-swap
victim will fail naive device checks while telling the truth. The
indicators justify investigation depth, not outcome.

## 4. Reporting discipline

4.1 Confirmed first-party fraud generates the same disclosure
questions as any other fraud, and reported matters show it
under-reported: the loss is absorbed as a dispute cost, the
customer exited quietly, and the conduct left invisible to the
next firm. The Authority's expectations for the quality and
completeness of suspicious activity reporting, including in
first-party matters, are set out in the guidance on suspicious
activity report quality at SG-06. A first-party actor exited
without a report simply resumes at a competitor, and the
goodwill-farming variant at §2.2 exists precisely because
sub-threshold conduct is assumed to go unshared.

## 5. Use of this report

5.1 Firms should test their dispute and reimbursement journeys
against §2, their investigation triggers against §3.1, and their
false-positive safeguards against §3.2 — the last with the same
seriousness as the detection itself. The measure of a first-party
fraud capability is not only the fraud it catches but the genuine
victims it clears quickly, and the Authority assesses both.

**End of TY-08.**
