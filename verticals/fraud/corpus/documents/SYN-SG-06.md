# SG-06 — Sector Guidance: Quality and Completeness of Suspicious Activity Reporting

**Issued by the Authority · Sector Guidance series · Reference SG-06**

## 1. Status and application

1.1 This guidance applies to relevant firms making suspicious
activity reports under the disclosure regime. It describes how the
Authority interprets the Framework's obligations as they bear on the
quality and completeness of those reports, and what it will look for
in supervisory work. It is guidance, not rules: a firm may meet the
underlying obligations by other means, but should expect to explain
how.

1.2 Firms should be aware that guidance issued by a supervisory
authority is among the matters a court must consider in proceedings
under section 330 of the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 (the extract at
OGL-0004): see s.330(8). Where the disclosure obligation arises, it
is discharged by a disclosure of the matters section 330(5)
describes; a report whose content falls short of that description
has not discharged it well, whatever was filed. This guidance is
written with that context in mind. It is not a statement of a firm's
obligations under that Act, and firms should take their own advice
on them.

1.3 This guidance concerns the report itself: what it contains and
when it is made. The internal escalation of suspicions to the
officer who reports, and the threshold at which reporting
obligations arise, are addressed elsewhere in the Framework.

## 2. The risk this guidance addresses

2.1 A suspicious activity report is the mechanism by which one
firm's knowledge becomes the system's. The firm that reports sees
one actor at one institution; the recipient of well-made reports can
see the actor's pattern across institutions. A report that is
absent, late, or empty of usable content fails not only the
reporting firm's obligation but the next firm the actor approaches.

2.2 The typology work at TY-08 records how this failure operates in
practice in first-party matters: the loss absorbed as a dispute
cost, the customer exited quietly, the conduct left invisible to the
next firm, the actor resuming at a competitor. The variant described
at TY-08 §2.2 exists precisely because sub-threshold conduct is
assumed to go unshared. Under-reporting of this kind is not an
accident of workload; it is a structural consequence of treating
reporting as a cost with no return to the reporting firm — and every
unmade report is borne, with interest, by the next firm the actor
approaches.

## 3. Completeness

3.1 Completeness begins with the statute. Section 330(5) of the Act
describes the required disclosure: the identity of the person, so
far as known; the whereabouts of the laundered property, so far as
known; and the information or other matter on which the knowledge or
suspicion, or the reasonable grounds for it, are based. A report
that withholds information the firm holds under any of those heads
is not a version of compliance; it is a shortfall against the
disclosure the obligation requires.

3.2 First-party matters are within the reporting discipline, and the
Authority draws attention to TY-08 §4 on this point specifically.
Confirmed first-party fraud generates the same disclosure questions
as any other fraud; resolution of the customer relationship —
reimbursement refused, account exited — resolves the firm's dispute,
not its reporting position.

3.3 The information grounding a suspicion most often first exists,
in objective form, in the firm's monitoring output. The escalation
path from monitoring to the reporting officer is the subject of
expectations at SG-03 §5; the completeness consequence is this
guidance's concern: a report that omits what the firm's monitoring
actually showed — the events observed, the pattern that fired, the
account behaviour that departed from baseline — is incomplete in the
way that matters most to the report's usefulness, whatever else it
contains. The report should carry the monitoring pattern that formed
the grounds, not merely a conclusion the firm drew from it. Where
monitoring has not generated the output a complete report would draw
on, the absence is itself a fact for the reporting decision: a
suspicion may still arise on the basis of what the firm otherwise
knows, and the firm should be able to explain why its monitoring did
not surface the matter.

## 4. Quality

4.1 A report is written for a reader who knows nothing about the
matter and must act on the report alone. Quality is measured against
that reader. The conduct should be described, not coded: what was
observed, over what period, involving which accounts and
counterparties, and why it departed from the expected. A report that
states a typology label where an account of the conduct should be
gives the reader a conclusion without the material to use it. A
report that says "suspected first-party fraud" tells the reader the
firm's classification. A report that says the customer has disputed
as unauthorised a transaction made from their enrolled device, at
their usual location, within a session showing their established
behavioural patterns; that this is their third such claim across the
firm's products in fourteen months; and that they declined to
support a police report — tells the reader what happened. The latter
is the standard this guidance expects.

4.2 For first-party matters, the indicator patterns at TY-08 §3
provide the vocabulary of what the firm observed; a quality report
states which indicators were present and what they looked like in
this case, rather than asserting the pattern in the abstract.

4.3 Timeliness is a dimension of quality, not a separate virtue. The
disclosure regime's value decays with distance from the conduct: a
technically complete report filed long after the firm's suspicion
crystallised has degraded as intelligence and invites the question
of what the firm was doing in the interval.

## 5. Supervisory consequence and records

5.1 The quality and completeness of a firm's reporting is
examinable, and the Authority examines it: in thematic work, in
firm-specific supervision, and in the aftermath of matters where
reporting failures come to light. Where supervisory work identifies
a reporting failure, what the Authority weighs in deciding its
response is set out at DP-01 §2, and the pathway from finding to
formal action at DP-01 §3. Where the failing identified is one of
capability rather than candour, the remediation route at DP-01 §4 is
available; a firm that has misdescribed its reporting position
should not expect it.

5.2 Firms should retain, for each report and for each matter
considered for reporting, records supporting later reconstruction:
what was known and when, what was reported and when, and — where a
matter was considered and not reported — the basis of that decision:
the suspicion or matter that was considered, the information the
firm held at the time, the person or function that reached the
conclusion, and the basis on which the threshold was judged not met.
The Authority's first questions of a firm whose reporting is
challenged will concern the distance between what its systems showed
and what its reports said.
